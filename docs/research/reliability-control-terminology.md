# Control and emergence terminology for reliability behavior

Research date: 2026-09-17. Scope: primary research and author-maintained technical material relevant to temporal behavior symbols. This is a bounded literature investigation, not an exhaustive review or novelty determination.

## Strongest direct taxonomy precedent

Jeffrey Mogul's *Emergent (Mis)behavior vs. Complex Software Systems* (EuroSys 2006; HP report HPL-2006-2), §5.1, proposes these categories: **thrashing, unwanted synchronization, unwanted oscillation or periodicity, deadlock, livelock, phase change, and chaotic behavior**. It explicitly separates these from component faults. Section 5.2 separately proposes classifying causes; §5.3 separates recognizing behavior from diagnosing its cause. This is a proposed taxonomy/research agenda, not a standardized or empirically complete ontology. A particularly relevant example alternates traffic between servers: the loaded server times out while the unloaded one appears recovered. None need have failed individually. [Primary paper, §§2.4, 5.1–5.3](https://shiftleft.com/mirrors/www.hpl.hp.com/techreports/2006/HPL-2006-2.pdf)

## Other established frameworks and terms

| Source/framework | What it actually establishes | Consequence for proposed symbols |
|---|---|---|
| Nonlinear dynamical systems | Equilibria, stability/asymptotic stability, and periodic trajectories/limit cycles are formal dynamical concepts. An attracting periodic trajectory can be stable; oscillation is therefore not synonymous with instability. | Use **observed oscillation** before making a mechanistic claim of a limit cycle. Separate bounded sustained, decaying, and growing oscillations, and distinguish undesirable behavior from mathematical instability. [Åström and Murray, author-hosted *Feedback Systems*, Ch. 4](https://www.cds.caltech.edu/~murray/books/AM08/pdf/fbs-public_22Jun14.pdf) |
| Switched/hybrid systems | Liberzon treats **chattering** as infinitely fast switching in an idealized model, with rapid switching as the physical manifestation. **Hysteresis switching** prevents chattering by separating switching boundaries and retaining discrete-state memory. | Rapid scale/state reversals may be analogous to chattering, but ordinary periodic oscillation and formal chattering should not be collapsed into one label. Hysteresis is a mechanism or mitigation, not itself a failure. [Liberzon, *Switched Systems: Stability Analysis and Control Synthesis*, §§2.3–2.4](https://liberzon.csl.illinois.edu/teaching/Liberzon-LectureNotes.pdf) |
| Kubernetes autoscaling | Official HPA documentation calls frequent replica-count fluctuation **thrashing** or **flapping** and describes stabilization windows and tolerance as mitigations. | Prefer **autoscaling flapping** to an invented label such as scale-up/scale-down hunting when describing the observable deployment behavior. Do not assume the cause is controller coupling merely because replicas alternate. This is operational terminology, not a general taxonomy. [Kubernetes HPA documentation, “Stability of workload scale”](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/) |
| Controller interaction in data-center power management | Raghavendra et al. classify coordination problems by **overlapping objectives, overlapping actuators, different time constants, and different problem formulations**. They describe correctness, stability, and efficiency consequences and evaluate coordinated management. | This is a classification of interaction mechanisms/design conflicts, not a catalog of observable time-series shapes. Useful evidence includes controller identity, objective, actuator, action time, and reaction delay. [*No “Power” Struggles*, ASPLOS 2008 / HP report, §2.3](https://shiftleft.com/mirrors/www.hpl.hp.com/techreports/2007/HPL-2007-194.pdf) |
| Routing convergence and policy conflict | Griffin, Shepherd, and Wilfong formalize the **Stable Paths Problem**, **safety** (guaranteed convergence), **robustness**, and **dispute wheels**. Policy conflicts can cause persistent route oscillation. Absence of a dispute wheel is sufficient for their safety result; presence does not alone demonstrate an observed oscillation. | A strong example of local preferences producing global nonconvergence. The paper warns that flapping caused by policy conflicts can look like flapping caused by defective equipment; additional history is needed to distinguish them. These formal results are specific to the model, not automatically general controller theorems. [*The Stable Paths Problem and Interdomain Routing*, 2002, §§IV–VII](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dragnet/15744/papers/stablepaths.pdf) |
| Synchronization in network populations | Floyd and Jacobson model **inadvertent synchronization** of apparently independent periodic routing processes. Their analysis and simulations show abrupt transition to synchronization and investigate randomization sufficient to prevent it. | Strong precedent for recognizing population-level temporal relations rather than isolated component anomalies. **Synchronization** is broader and better established than “synchronized remediation”; remediation can be an application-specific action type. One coincident event is weaker evidence than recurring phase alignment. [*The Synchronization of Periodic Routing Messages*, 1994](https://www.icir.org/floyd/papers/sync_94.pdf) |

## Direct cloud-controller precedent and caveat

Bryan Ford's *Icebergs in the Clouds* (HotCloud 2012), §2, describes unintended coupling of independently developed reactive controllers. In its example, a load balancer and a power controller each work under assumptions that the other violates. Their simultaneous corrections overcompensate, producing increasingly large workload swings. The paper also discusses hidden dependencies and correlated failures. **It explicitly labels its argument speculative and forward-looking**, so it supports prior articulation of the risk, not prevalence or a validated detector. [Primary paper, §§1–3](https://dedis.cs.yale.edu/cloud/papers/hotcloud12-icebergs.pdf)

## Progression from industrial control to modern cluster verification

Parunak and VanderBok's *Managing Emergent Behavior in Distributed Control Systems* (ISA-Tech 1997) distinguishes **fixed points, oscillation, and formal chaos**, separately discussing capacity limits, feedback, and delays as sources of nonlinearity. Its welding example has controllers wait for voltage recovery, then fire together and depress voltage again: collective recovery decisions recreate overload. Its parallel-pump example has load transfer, sediment clearing, successive overload shutdowns, and recurrence after restart. These are useful precedents for the user's proposed labels without establishing those labels as canonical terms. The paper treats observed patterns as hypotheses to investigate using system models and simulation. Its manufacturing examples support cross-domain analogy, not a claim that identical dynamics occur in this benchmark. [Original paper text reproduced by StudyLib, pp. 2–7](https://studylib.net/doc/8298729/managing-emergent-behavior-in-distributed-control-systems); bibliographic identity corroborated by the [author's publication catalog](https://www.abcresearch.org/abc/papers/), entry 163.

**Kivi: Verification for Cluster Management** (USENIX ATC 2024) provides a modern, domain-specific **property taxonomy**: unexpected topology, object numbers, object lifecycles, and oscillation. It calls the first three safety properties and oscillation a liveness property; categories can overlap. Separately, it classifies interactions within one controller, between controllers, and between controllers and events. Its C8 example cycles through descheduler eviction, deployment-controller replacement, and scheduler placement that recreates the problem. Kivi checks modeled interleavings, rather than recognizing patterns in telemetry. Its study includes 16 selected cases, and its scaling procedure does not guarantee zero false negatives. [Primary paper, §§3–4, Tables 2–3](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc24-liu-bingzhe.pdf)

**Our inference:** finite telemetry showing repeated eviction/replacement establishes recurrence within an observed interval, not an infinite liveness violation. A symbolic record should say `observed_cycles=3`, name the interval, and preserve actor/action/object relationships. It should reserve a nonconvergence claim for stronger evidence under an explicit model and assumptions.

## Implications for the thesis — our synthesis, not a source taxonomy

A useful representation should preserve three distinct kinds of claims:

1. **Observed temporal relation:** replica count repeatedly reverses; traffic shifts A→B→A; two controllers act in phase; throughput declines while offered work rises.
2. **Behavior classification:** autoscaling flapping, unwanted synchronization, oscillation, or thrashing, with explicit evidence and interval.
3. **Mechanistic hypothesis:** overlapping actuators, delayed feedback, policy conflict, or load-balancer/health-check interaction. This requires more evidence than the shape alone.

Thus the thesis is not adequately tested by distinguishing “high” from “rising.” A better independent assertion is: **compression preserves the temporal and relational evidence necessary to distinguish an interaction-driven behavior from a lookalike with a different explanation.** For example, controller-driven replica oscillation and legitimate tracking of periodic demand can share a replica-count trace. Demand, decisions, and their ordering distinguish the accounts. An LLM receiving only `OSCILLATION` has been given a conclusion; one receiving compact, timed evidence can evaluate alternatives.

Do not use **chaos**, **limit cycle**, **instability**, or **chattering** interchangeably with noisy or wavy telemetry. Do not treat **thrashing** as having one universal meaning: resource-switching overhead and replica flapping use the word at different levels. Avoid claiming a universal reliability-behavior taxonomy exists on the strength of these sources; what exists here is a directly relevant proposed taxonomy plus rigorous domain-specific frameworks and operational vocabulary.

The HP papers are original primary-source reports accessed through a mirror because the historical HP URLs are obsolete; publication metadata for the power-management paper is independently listed by [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/no-power-struggles-coordinated-multi-level-power-management-for-the-data-center/).
